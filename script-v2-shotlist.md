## ACT 1 — HOOK (0:00–0:28) · C1–C3

**C1** — Slow aerial push toward a still, mirror-flat crater lake at golden hour, steep green walls, total silence.
**C2** — Cut to ground level: bare feet on a dirt path, a boy driving cattle along the ridge above the lake.
**C3** — The boy stops, looks back at the water. Freeze on his face. Title flickers: *AUGUST 21, 1986*.

**NARRATOR:**
See that lake? It looks like a postcard. In one night, it killed one thousand
seven hundred people — without a flood, without a volcano, without a single
warning shot. It's August 21st, 1986, in the highlands of Cameroon. You're
twelve years old, herding your family's cattle home above Lake Nyos. And in
nine hours, you'll be one of the only people in your village still breathing.

## ACT 2 — CALM & SIGNS (0:28–0:57) · C4–C7

**C4** — Village at dusk: cooking fires, kids chasing chickens, laughter, warm light.
**C5** — Detail shot: the lake's surface — a patch of water fizzing with fine bubbles, like an opened soda.
**C6** — An old fisherman frowns at the bubbles, then shrugs and casts his net anyway.
**C7** — Low rumble SFX; a cup of water on a table ripples. Nobody looks up.

**NARRATOR:**
The valley is good land. Rich soil, fat cattle, three villages tucked along the
lake's edge. But the lake has been… whispering. Fishermen talk about patches of
water that fizz like something's boiling far below. Some evenings, the ground
gives a low growl. The elders say the lake has a bad spirit. Everyone else says
lakes don't kill people. Tonight, both of them are wrong about how.

## ACT 3 — THE ERUPTION (0:57–1:40) · C8–C12

**C8** — Night. The lake dead calm under a half moon. A deep BOOM; birds explode from the trees.
**C9** — The lake's center bulges, then erupts — a column of water and foam blasting 300 feet up, moonlit.
**C10** — Slow-motion: the fountain collapses into a churning white ring spreading across the surface.
**C11** — A wall of pale mist rises off the water, taller than the trees, swallowing the shoreline.
**C12** — The boy on the ridge wakes in his blanket, sits up, sees the white mass filling the valley below.

**NARRATOR:**
9 PM. A boom rolls through the hills like the earth clearing its throat. The
middle of the lake rises — and then it erupts. Not lava. Water. A fountain
punches three hundred feet into the night sky. For decades, this crater lake
has been quietly loading itself like a weapon: volcanic vents beneath it
leaking carbon dioxide into the deep water, drop by drop, until the bottom of
Lake Nyos held more gas than water. Tonight, something — a landslide, a shift,
no one knows — pulled the trigger. The lake doesn't overflow. It *exhales*.

## ACT 4 — THE CLOUD (1:40–2:04) · C13–C15

**C13** — Aerial: a ghostly, ground-hugging white river of mist pours over the crater rim into the valleys.
**C14** — The cloud rolls downhill through a cattle field; cows drop mid-stride, one by one.
**C15** — Torches in a village below wink out as the mist reaches them, like candles being pinched.

**NARRATOR:**
A hundred thousand tons of carbon dioxide burst out of the water in minutes.
CO2 is invisible, has almost no smell — and it's heavier than air. So it
doesn't drift away. It *flows*. A fifty-foot-deep river of suffocating gas
pours over the crater rim and rushes down the valleys at forty-five miles an
hour, hugging the ground, pushing the oxygen out from underneath the sleeping
villages. You can't outrun it. You can't see it. You can only be above it.

## ACT 5 — THE DECISION (2:04–2:48) · C16–C20

**C16** — The boy scrambles uphill, dragging his little sister by the wrist, cattle bellowing behind.
**C17** — Below, a family runs the other way — downhill, toward relatives, into the white.
**C18** — A single motorcycle headlight cuts through the dark, descending the valley road into the mist.
**C19** — The headlight wobbles, slows… and tips over. The beam shines sideways into fog. Engine stops.
**C20** — On the ridge, the boy and his sister collapse in the grass, gasping — above the cloud line. Hold.

**NARRATOR:**
Now comes the decision nobody knows they're making. The gas fills the valleys
like water fills a bowl — so every footstep uphill is a breath earned, and
every step downhill is a breath spent. You grab your sister and climb, because
the cattle are climbing, and some old instinct says *follow them*. Others run
downhill to warn family. A man on a motorcycle rides toward Nyos village to
help. He makes it half a mile into the low ground. The ones who climbed watch
his headlight go still. Elevation. That's the whole game. Fifty vertical feet
decide who sees morning.

## ACT 6 — NIGHT OF SILENCE (2:48–3:30) · C21–C25

**C21** — Wide night shot: the mist river forks through three valleys, reaching villages miles apart.
**C22** — Inside a hut: a mother mid-motion putting a pot down — frame slowly desaturates, sound fades out.
**C23** — A dog lies at a doorway as the mist slides past, thin as morning fog now, almost gentle.
**C24** — TOTAL SILENCE (no music, no SFX): the valley under moonlight, white haze settling like snow.
**C25** — Dawn. The boy wakes on the ridge. Below: villages intact, smoke gone, nothing moving at all.

**NARRATOR:**
The cloud doesn't stop at Nyos. It rides the valleys ten, fifteen miles out,
into Cha, into Subum, into homes where people never even woke up. There's no
fire. No collapse. No screaming. Carbon dioxide at these concentrations takes
a person in two or three breaths — people fall mid-step, mid-sentence,
mid-dream.
*(beat — narration stops for C24, four full seconds of silence)*
At dawn, you wake up on the ridge. The valley looks completely untouched. The
houses are standing. The fields are green. And nothing — nothing — is moving.

## ACT 7 — THE END (3:30–3:56) · C26–C28

**C26** — The boy walks down into the village, past cattle lying in the fields like sleeping statues.
**C27** — Through a doorway: a family around a cold cooking pot, frozen where they sat. A candle, unburned, tipped over.
**C28** — Slow overhead pull-back: the village, then the valley, then the lake — now rust-brown, not blue.

**NARRATOR:**
One thousand seven hundred forty-six people. Three and a half thousand cattle.
Whole families exactly where the night left them — no wounds, no struggle,
lamps and candles snuffed out by the same gas that took their air. Survivors
wake confused after hours unconscious, some having outlived everyone they've
ever known. And behind it all, the lake sits quiet again — except it's changed
color. It's red.

## ACT 8 — DISCOVERY (3:56–4:24) · C29–C31

**C29** — Days later: helicopters, scientists in field gear at the shoreline, sampling the rust-colored water.
**C30** — Macro shot: a sealed water sample fizzing violently as it's brought up from depth.
**C31** — Whiteboard-style animated diagram: CO2 seeping into deep water, layer loading, overturn, exhale.

**NARRATOR:**
Scientists arrive to a mystery with no precedent: seventeen hundred dead and
not a mark on the land. The red water gives the first clue — iron, dragged up
from the lake's bottom, rusting in the air. The deep water fizzes like
champagne when they raise a sample. The verdict is a phenomenon almost no one
had ever named: a limnic eruption. A lake so still, so deep, so gas-loaded,
that it detonated. Only one other has ever been recorded — Lake Monoun, two
years earlier, twenty-five miles away. Nobody had connected the dots.

## ACT 9 — TWIST & OUTRO (4:24–5:00) · C32–C35

**C32** — Present day: a thin white geyser sprays from a raft in the lake's center — the degassing pipe.
**C33** — Aerial pull-back from Nyos… map zoom out… south to a vastly bigger lake ringed by city lights.
**C34** — Night shot: Lake Kivu shoreline, millions of lights, fishermen's lanterns on the black water.
**C35** — Final card over the still lake: *THE LAKE IS STILL LOADING.* Subscribe prompt.

**NARRATOR:**
Here's the part they don't put on the postcard. Lake Nyos started reloading
the day after the disaster — the vents never stopped. Today, engineered pipes
run to the lake floor, venting the gas in a permanent fountain to keep it from
building again. But six hundred miles south sits Lake Kivu: a thousand times
more gas… and over two million people living on its shores. Scientists watch
it every single day. If you want the story of the lake that could do this
again — that's the next video. Don't get caught downhill.
